ABSTRACT
For resource utilization efficiency, latency critical (LC) services
are commonly co-located with best-effort batch jobs in datacenter
servers. Many LC services, such as Cassandra and HBase, run in
Java Virtual Machine (JVM). We find that LC services often ex-
perience heavy-tailed latency due to performance interference of
the concurrent garbage collection (GC) as well as multi-tenancy.
The root cause is a semantic gap of resource allocation between
JVM and the underlying Linux OS in multi-tenant systems. That
is, the OS is unaware of the characteristics of different kinds of
threads in JVM (i.e., GC threads and LC worker threads), which
may lead to GC threads competing for CPUs; JVM is unaware of the
resource utilization in the OS, which may trigger CPU-intensive
GC operations when CPUs are busy. Furthermore, we find that
co-located batch jobs can interfere with LC services due to Simulta-
neous Multi-Threading (SMT).
We propose iGC, a middleware that bridges the semantic gap
between JVM and Linux OS and improves concurrent GC perfor-
mance in multi-tenant systems. iGC adaptively triggers GC based
on the CPU utilization at runtime, which speeds up the GC process
and reduces its CPU contention. Furthermore, iGC deploys a dy-
namic CPU scheduling and thread placement strategy to avoid or
mitigate the interference due to concurrent GC and multi-tenancy,
but also improve the cache performance. We implement iGC upon
two state-of-the-art concurrent GC mechanisms ZGC and G1 GC.
We conduct its evaluation using three NoSQL databases as LC ser-
vices. Experimental results show that iGC significantly improves
the performance of concurrent GC for LC services and the through-
put in multi-tenant systems. iGC reduces the p95 tail latency by
83%, 37% and 22% for the three LC services Cassandra, HBase and
Solr, respectively. It also increases the throughput of LC services
up to 2.56X.

1 INTRODUCTION
For resource efficiency in cloud computing, latency critical (LC)
services such as key-value stores and web search are commonly co-
located with best-effort batch jobs in datacenter servers. However,
as applications are co-located and contend for shared resources,
multi-tenancy often leads to severe interference and unpredictable
performance, especially the heavy-tailed latency for LC services.
The heavy-tailed latency problem is significant because cloud
applications are undergoing a major redesign from large, mono-
lithic services to hundreds or thousands of loosely-coupled mi-
croservices [6, 25]. A user request of LC services often triggers
sub-requests to multiple components across many servers and the
end-to-end response time is largely determined by the slowest in-
dividual latency of the sub-requests [17]. As LC services need to
maintain stable and low latency for user requests, the problem
significantly impacts the QoS of cloud computing.
There are growing interests that aim to improve the tail latency
of LC services from the perspectives of workload placement [55],
CPU isolation between LC services and batch jobs [31], tail-aware
caching[4],andresourcepartitioning[6,36].Recently,Caladan[24]
proposes a new Linux OS scheduler that reduces the tail latency
in multi-tenant systems via fast core allocation instead of resource
partitioning. Hermes [46] and Perséphone [20] design reservation-
based memory allocation and kernel-bypass OS scheduling ap-
proaches to minimize the tail latency for LC services executing at
the microsecond scale, respectively.
In this paper, we tackle the problem of heavy-tailed latency
of LC services from two new aspects. First, we identify a new
interference causing the heavy-tailed latency in JVM-based LC

overhead of GC. Traditional GC in JVM leads to the Stop The World

The root cause is a semantic gap of resource allocation between
JVM and the Linux OS in multi-tenant systems. 1) JVM is unaware
of the available resources in the OS. It triggers GC only based on
the status of JVM, which often prolongs the execution time of both
GC threads and threads of batch jobs that share CPUs with the GC

threads in JVM, which may lead to threads competing for the same
set of logical CPUs. In such a case, GC threads contend for CPU
resourcewithworkerthreadsofLCservicesinJVM,whichprolongs
the latency of requests of LC services.
Second, servers enable Simultaneous Multi-Threading (SMT)
technology to improve system throughput. Linux OS may schedule
different workloads on the logical CPUs running on one physical
corebySMTtechnology.Asaresult,co-locatedbatchjobscanincur
non-trivial SMT interference with LC services, leading to cache
contention and longer request latency of LC services.

betweenJVMandtheOSandimprovesconcurrentGCperformance
in multi-tenant systems. 1) iGC adaptively triggers GC based on the
available CPU resource at runtime, which speeds up GC process
and reduces its CPU contention. 2) iGC deploys a dynamic CPU
scheduling and thread placement strategy to avoid or mitigate the
interference due to concurrent GC and multi-tenancy, but also
improve the cache performance at the same time.
Specifically,iGCseparatesGCthreadsfromworkerthreadsofLC
services by assigning different CPUs for the GC threads and worker
threads, avoiding the interference. To mitigate SMT interference,
iGC tries to assign threads of batch jobs and worker threads of
LC services to different cores. Further, iGC limits the resource
usage of batch threads when GC threads are co-located with batch
threads and GC is triggered. As a result, it effectively mitigates the
contention of memory hierarchy in a multi-tenant system.
We implement iGC as a middleware upon two state-of-the-art
concurrent GC mechanisms, ZGC and G1 GC. We conduct per-
formance evaluation using three representative NoSQL databases
Cassandra, HBase, and Solr as the LC services and Spark KMeans as
the co-located batch jobs. Experimental results show that compared
to ZGC, iGC reduces the p95 latency by 83%, 37% and 22% for Cas-
sandra, HBase, and Solr, respectively. It reduces the L1 and L2 cache
miss rate by 55.7% and 54.4%, respectively. It also increases the
instruction throughput of the LC services up to 2.56X. Compared
to G1 GC, iGC reduces the p95 latency by 78% and improves the
instruction throughput up to 1.45X for Cassandra.

2 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATIONS
2.1 Concurrent GC Interference
Though concurrent GC is effective in mitigating the STW overhead,
GC threads compete CPU resources with other threads which can
severely increase the latency of LC services and reduce the system
throughput. In a multi-tenant system, GC threads compete with
threads of multiple types of workloads. 1) Different threads within
one JVM compete for CPUs, that is, concurrent GC threads compete
with worker threads, which prolongs the request latency of the LC
service. 2) Threads of batch jobs (batch threads) in one JVM share
and compete for CPUs with GC threads of a LC service in another
JVM, which prolongs the GC process.
To study the impact of concurrent GC on latency of LC services,
we run Cassandra, a NoSQL database, as the LC service on Java 11
with ZGC. We use YCSB [12] as the benchmark to send requests to
theLCservice.TheexperimentalsetupissameasthatinSection5.1.
We compare the default ZGC and ZGC with adaptive thread place-
ment. For the default ZGC, GC threads may be scheduled on the
same set of CPUs that host worker threads of Cassandra. For the
ZGC with adaptive thread placement, GC threads are assigned to
CPUs that do not host worker threads of Cassandra.
Figure 1(a) shows the latency of the Read and Update operations
of Cassandra at runtime. Compared to ZGC with adaptive thread
placement, the default ZGC leads to more frequent GC activities
and longer GC time. The reason is that worker threads and GC
threads share the same set of CPUs, leading to severe contention.
The longest latencies are due to the ZGC stalls when there is not
enough memory on the JVM heap for allocation before the GC is
finished. Separating ZGC threads and placing them onto CPUs that

2.2 SMT/Hyper-Threading Interference
SMT has been widely enabled in modern multi-core processors. It
improves the system throughput by scheduling instructions from
multiple hardware threads (each is called a logical CPU) in one
physical core, which can concurrently utilize different execution
units on the core. In this paper, we target Intel’s implementation
of SMT, which is named Hyper-Threading (HT). We use CPU to
represent logical CPU and core to represent physical core. HT
implements a 2-way hardware threads on a physical core. Two
CPUs on a core share the same pipeline and L1, L2 caches.
While HT technique improves the resource efficiency, it also
incurs non-trivial overhead to the both hardware threads on the
corefortworeasons.1)Thecoreisunawareofthehardwarethreads
from which instructions come. There is not a mechanism that can
prioritize a thread with a better opportunity for occupying the
pipeline. When two threads run on the two CPUs of the same core,
each thread executes instructions slower compared to that one

of the execution units and contention in the memory hierarchy.
Summary.Wesummarizetheidentifiedissuesasbelow.1)Garbage
Collection is a CPU intense operation. Thus, it should be adaptively
triggered based on the CPU resource at runtime. 2) Although con-
current GC reduces the stall of applications, it leads to more CPU
interference as it runs in parallel with worker threads of the same
process. 3) Assigning GC threads and batch threads on the CPUs of
the same core severely degrades the tail latency of LC services due
to CPU contention and HT interference.

based on the available CPU resource at runtime to speeds up GC
procedure and reduce its CPU contention. It monitors the CPU
usage of servers and dynamically adjusts the time interval between
GC operations based on the heap usage of the JVM. 2) Dynamic
CPU allocation and thread placement. iGC deploys a simple yet

Figure 4 shows the architecture of iGC. iGC consists of three
units: 1) Monitor that obtains server resource information, i.e.,
heap size of the target application, CPU utilization for each core
on the server. It uses samples to calculate the speed at which the
heap size grows. 2) Short term controller that triggers GC based
on the information from the other two units. It also limits the
resources used by the batch threads when necessary. 3) Long term
controller that decides the parameters related to GC triggering,
such as thresholds of heap size, CPU utilization and time interval of
GC, and importantly CPU allocation and thread placement. A JVM
is the runtime for a LC service. It contains worker threads and GC
threads. In addition, there are batch threads for batch jobs. There
are five thread placement scenarios on the two CPUs of a core, i.e.,
worker and worker, batch and batch, worker and GC, worker and
batch with GC, and batch and batch with GC. In case of virtualized
cloud environments, each virtual machine (VM) hosts one iGC that
makes GC triggering and CPU scheduling decisions for the hosted
applications. Each VM can access multiple cores on the server. Note
that scenario 3 only occurs when resources are plenty, otherwise, it
will be transformed to scenario 4 or 5 for higher resource utilization.
3.1 Monitor
The monitor periodically collects the resource utilization informa-
tion including CPU usage and heap usage of a target JVM. The CPU
usage is obtained from the file system in Linux OS. The heap usage
of the JVM is obtained through the Java Management Extensions
(JMX) framework introduced since Java 1.5 [15]. JMX is an out-of-
the-box management tool for monitoring and managing JVM. The
monitor also tracks the allocation of objects on the Java heap and
computes the allocation rate. The obtained information is sent to
the short-term controller and the long-term controller.
3.2 Short Term Controller
The controller manages the adaptive GC triggering procedure. The
logic is shown in Algorithm 1. It triggers GC based on CPU usage,
heapusage,andintervalsetbyMonitorandthelongtermcontroller.
ItlimitstheCPUusageofbatchthreadsontheallocatedCPUwhere
GC threads are running (scenarios 4 and 5 in Figure 4). One naive
way is to remove the allocated CPU from the affinity list of batch
threads. Here, we choose to set low priority in CPU usage for batch
threads so they can be resumed when GC is finished.
3.2.1 HowtotriggerGC. iGCdecideshowtotriggerGCatruntime.
Instead of modifying the JVM directly, we access JVM information
and control GC via JMX. JMX obtains the status of Java heap and
triggers GC via call system.gc() from outside of the JVM. Algo-
rithm 1 shows the operations when GC is triggered. By default, GC
threads may share a CPU with batch threads. If the JVM is started
with root privilege, GC is triggered directly as the OS scheduler will
treat it with high priority. If not, GC is triggered if cpu_usage of the
CPU where GC threads run is smaller than cpu_threshold, which is
empirically set to 70% to reduce the CPU contention between GC
and batch threads. Note that with iGC, the GC CPU usage on each
assigned CPU is around 30%. To speed up GC operations, we set GC
threads with a high priority on the CPUs where GC is triggered. If
the CPU resource is not enough, iGC limits the CPU usage of batch
threads sharing the same CPU with GC threads by cgroup [11].

3.2.2 When to trigger GC. There are two conditions for the timing
of triggering GC, 1) There is enough CPU resource so it proactively
triggers GC, and 2) the heap usage increases beyond a threshold
such that GC must be triggered to avoid the allocation stall. iGC
keeps monitoring the heap usage of the JVM and the CPU usage of
the server. It periodically checks whether GC should be triggered
by a dynamic time interval I and two thresholds. Threshold A is
a threshold for the heap usage, below which GC is not triggered
as there is enough heap space for allocation. iGC tries to trigger
GC when heap_usage is larger than threshold A. In this case, if
heap_usageisnotlargerthanthresholdB,ittriggersGCifcpu_usage
is smaller than cpu_threshold. If heap_usage is larger than threshold
B, GC is triggered regardless of the value of cpu_usage. Using two
thresholds reduces the interference to batch threads. To ensure
iGC is properly working, the default concurrent GC is disabled.
Note when the heap size reaches a very high level between iGC
samplings, the JVM can still trigger GC to avoid the stall of LC
services.

3.3 Long Term Controller
This unit has two key functions, dynamic CPU allocation and
thread placement and adaptive monitoring interval. The former
addresses non-trivial CPU contentions between GC threads and
worker threads and between batch threads and worker threads. It
also leverages HT for cache performance improvement. The latter
controlsparametersusedinGC,includingtheheapusagethreshold,
the CPU usage threshold, and the number of GC threads.
3.3.1 Priority-based Dynamic Thread Placement. There is not a
thread scheduler in JVM itself. A JVM sets different priority levels
to Java threads and creates an OS-level thread for each Java thread.
The Linux OS schedules Java threads based on their priority.
JVM Thread Priority. All GC threads are initialized when a
JVM starts runtime. We use command jps to get process ID and
commandjstacktocapturethethreaddumpoftheJVMandobtain
thread labels and IDs. Table 1 shows the label and priority level
of all GC threads and representative worker threads in the JVM
of Cassandra. Note that ZGC and G1 GC use different labels for
GC threads. We can see that most of the worker threads have JVM
priority levels from 5 to 9, except for the last three threads that
have the highest priority level 10. All GC threads have the highest
JVM priority level 10. The last row of the table shows that all JVM
threads are assigned the priority level 0 by the OS when the JVM is
initiatedatuserspace.Asaresult,fortheJVMrunninginuserspace,
GC threads and worker threads are treated equally by the Linux OS
scheduler and the use of round-robin time-slicing based scheduling.
When the JVM is initiated with root privilege, the priority levels
of the threads are converted to the OS-level priority levels by the
JVM and the OS schedules the threads based on their priority.
Under both scenarios, the OS is incapable of avoiding or mitigat-
ing the interference between the GC threads and worker threads
by its scheduler itself. If GC threads are assigned with a higher
priority level than those of worker threads, worker threads will be
preempted or reduced time slice size when GC is triggered, which
prolongs the request latency. If GC threads are assigned with a
lower priority, it may lead to expensive STW problems or even
OutOfMemory errors.
iGC Dynamic Thread Placement Strategy. To avoid or mit-
igate the interference due to concurrent GC and job co-location
but also increase the cache performance, iGC places three types
of threads on each core with five scenarios in order, which are
illustrated at the left side of Figure 4. 1) It assigns both CPUs of one
core to worker threads, which avoids the interference from batch
threads and makes use of the memory hierarchy of the core. 2) It
assigns both CPUs of one core to batch threads, which avoids the
impact on worker threads. 3) When there are enough CPUs, worker
threads and GC threads are assigned to the two CPUs of one core,
respectively. 4) When CPUs are not enough, GC threads share the
CPU with batch threads on one CPU, while worker threads use the
other CPU. 5) In the worst scenario, GC threads share one CPU
with batch threads, while other batch threads use the other CPU of
the same core.
The CPU and thread placement is dynamic. Firstly, iGC places
worker threads and batch threads into different cores (scenarios 1
and 2). If the CPU usage of the LC service is higher than 90% during
a sampling interval, one more core is assigned to the LC service.
If there are more batch jobs and the CPU usage is heavy on batch
threads, iGC co-locates GC threads with batch threads on one CPU
(scenarios 4 and 5) by orchestrating with the Linux OS scheduler.
We discuss three possible co-location scenarios at the CPU level:
1) Worker threads and GC threads, 2) batch threads and GC threads,
and 3) Worker threads and batch threads.
Worker & GC Worker threads may compete with GC threads
when they are placed on the same CPU. However, Linux OS does
not distinguish the two types of threads at runtime. To address
the problem, we collect information of Java threads from the JVM
as each thread has its own label. Each Java thread in the JVM has
its corresponding system thread in Linux. Those system threads
share one process identification (PID). With the obtained labels,
iGC distinguishes the two types of threads and pins them onto
different CPUs, which avoids the contention between the worker
threads and GC threads.
Batch & GC Though batch threads compete resources with GC
threads, GC is not in the critical path of a LC service as the in-
terference on GC threads from batch threads will not impact the
latency of the LC service. GC threads are only active for a short
interval. As long as it finishes before the heap size reaches the limit,
there is not allocation stall. As a result, GC threads can share CPU
resources with batch threads. By starting JVMs with root privilege,
iGC co-locates GC threads with low-priority batch threads on one
CPU for high utilization. It is the Linux OS scheduler that sched-
ules the threads based on their priority. Alternatively, if JVMs are
started at user space, iGC prioritizes GC threads by the use of nice
command [27] to reduce the interference between batch threads
and GC threads.
Worker & Batch Batch threads compete CPU and memory hier-
archy resources with worker threads. Batch threads should not be
placed in CPUs that execute worker threads.
3.3.2 Making use of HT. Two factors are considered when making
use of the HT technique, the impact on the memory hierarchy and
on the instruction throughput. Sharing the same physical core leads
to contention on the core-level caches, i.e., L1 data cache, L1 code
cache, and L2 cache. Each process owns one virtual memory space.
47Middleware ’22, November 7–11, 2022, Quebec City, Quebec, Canada Junxian and Aidi, et al.
The data stored in caches can be reused by threads from the same
process. An efficient way that takes advantage of the HT technique
is to place worker threads on one CPU and GC threads on the
other CPU of the same physical core. When worker threads and
GC threads of the LC service share two different CPUs on the same
core, they can share the cached data. It reduces the access to the
lower levels of the memory hierarchy, i.e., LLC cache and the main
memory. In return, the fast data fetching reduces the stall in the
instruction pipeline and increases the instruction throughput as
well.

changed at runtime according to the heap allocation growth. It aims
to reduce the possibility of triggering the default GC while reduc-
ing the monitoring overhead. Algorithm 2 shows the procedure
of the adaptive monitoring interval. The interval of obtaining the
resource usage and heap usage varies in different configurations.
The frequency of GC is also dynamically changed based on the
allocation rate of new objects in the Java heap. The allocation rate
is calculated by the number of new objects allocated in Java heap
per second. iGC keeps sampling the allocation rate of new objects
and computes the new value of the interval.
In Algorithm 2, parameter u is the coefficient that determines
the weight of the new rate and the current rate in the new moni-
toring interval. The formula at line 6 is to get the new interval for
triggering GC. The new interval is calculated based on the current

The allocation rate is volatile in Java heap. Since it changes fre-
quently, we choose to give a higher weight for the new allocation
rate. To reduce the fluctuation from bursty heap allocations, the
value of u is empirically assigned to 0.2. As a result, the interval of

4 IMPLEMENTATION
iGC, implemented as a lightweight middleware between JVM and
Linux OS, is written in Bash and C++ with around 1,000 lines of
code. The implementation tackles several challenges. Firstly, its
monitor needs to collect information from the JVM, i.e., different
thread types, heap usage, object allocation rate, but also from the
Linux OS, i.e., CPU usage for each core. Secondly, iGC needs a
suitable interface for controlling JVM parameters at runtime. Last
but not least, iGC needs to be compatible with the two state-of-
the-art concurrent GC mechanisms ZGC and G1GC. iGC project is
open source at https://github.com/lotusknight/iGC.
iGC daemon process runs with the root privilege to access the
information of resource usages and control resource scheduling
on servers. It obtains CPU usage information from the Linux file
/proc/stat. It samples the CPU usage in each second.
JMX facilitates programmers to monitor and manage JVM. iGC
uses JMX to access the Java heap usage and trigger GC from outside
of the JVM. With JMX, iGC can be deployed for all Java applications
without modifying the source code of applications. When employ-
ing JMX, we assign JMX port number for each Java application
when starting the JVM.
iGC has interfaces to ZGC [16] and G1 GC [22]. Note that iGC
and JVM won’t trigger GC threads at the same time as iGC is a
middleware that triggers GC through JVM GC interface. Once iGC
has triggered GC, it will change a status flag in JVM so that no more
GC will be triggered at the same.
iGC uses scripts to obtain the PIDs of the JVMs for LC services
and batch jobs. It uses command jstack to capture the JVM thread
dump and obtain thread labels and thread IDs. Threads both related
to GC and worker are recognized as worker threads. With the labels
and thread IDs, iGC distinguishes the GC threads and places them
onto CPUs according to the priority-based dynamic thread place-
ment strategy. iGC sets the thread CPU affinity by using taskset
command. We use Linux cgroup to adjust the CPU limits for GC
threads and batch threads which share the same CPUs. iGC can
adapt to JVMs in both user space and root privilege. If JVMs are
started at user space, command nice() is used to set GC threads
with higher priority when they are co-located with batch threads to
reduce the CPU interference. If JVMs are started with root privilege,
iGC dynamic thread placement avoids or mitigates the interference.

5.7 Discussions
iGC Overhead. iGC separates GC threads from worker threads
and place them onto different CPUs. Compared with ZGC or G1
GC, iGC uses one more CPU to run GC threads. Note that overhead
of GC is limited at most one CPU in non-work-conserving manner
regardless of the cluster size. As GC threads are not in the critical
path of LC services, GC threads can share CPUs with threads of
batch jobs. Additionally, GC threads only consume CPU resource
when GC is triggered. In our evaluation, the CPU utilization of GC
threads is around 30% of a CPU when GC is running. As long as
iGC avoids allocation stall, its overhead is less than the capacity of
one logical CPU.

6 RELATED WORK
LC service Optimization. Many studies focus on improving the
QoS of LC services from the perspective of reducing tail latency [4,
20, 24, 46, 55]. For example, PerfIso [31] uses native CPU isolation
to achieve CPU sharing between LC services and best-effort batch
jobs. FlatStore [10] is a key-value store that uses logs in persis-
tent memory for serving data requests. Caladan [24] proposes a
new CPU scheduler in the Linux kerneal that improves QoS of
LC services, especially the tail latency. Perséphone [20] is a novel
kernel-bypass OS scheduler designed to minimize the tail latency
for applications executing at the microsecond scale.
GC Optimization. There are studies on JVM optimization [5, 22,
28, 28, 38, 40, 43, 44, 52, 54]. For example, Facade [44] implements
a compiler framework, which automatically stores data to native
storage. ROLP [5] profiles object lifetime and uses the profiled infor-
mationforefficientGC.Recently,Semeru[51]providesadistributed
GC that offloads object tracing to memory servers so that tracing is
performed closer to data in memory-disaggregated systems. Other
studies focus on reducing the overhead of object serialization and
caching intermediate data in memory. Deca [37] transforms ap-
plication code to access native storage directly for performance
improvement. Skyway [42] and FlashByte [54] propose to transfer
objects from heap to native storage, which alleviates the serializa-
tion overhead. Gerenuk [41] is a compiler and runtime that aims
to enable a JVM-based data-parallel system to achieve near native
efficiency.
Resource Management in Multi-tenant Systems. Many stud-
ies [8–10, 23, 26, 32] focus on job scheduling and memory man-
agement in multi-tenant systems. For instance, HipsterCo [45] is
a task scheduler for LC services running on multi-core systems.
BIG-C [9] is a preemptive, low latency datacenter scheduling frame-
workvialightweightcontainers.iTask[23]andUAkiller[7]forcibly
suspend tasks and performs node-level memory management to
prioritize specific tasks. Hermes [46] offers fast memory allocation
and proactive reclamation for reducing the latency of LC services
under memory pressure. It targets applications written in C. iGC
is an adaptive CPU allocation and thread placement approach that
leverages on concurrent GC at runtime to tackle performance in-
terference and reduce the tail latency of LC services. It targets Java
applications that require GC.
PerformanceInterferenceandIsolation.Priorworkhastackled
performance interference in cloud systems in several ways. Some
approaches disallow interactive services from sharing resources
withotherapplicationstoavoidinterference[36,47,50].Someother
approaches try to avoid co-scheduling applications that are likely
to interfere with each other [18, 39]. The third category of research
focus on eliminating interference altogether, by partitioning and
isolating resources among co-scheduled services [19, 34–36, 46].
Parties [6] is a representative QoS-aware resource manager that
enables a number of interactive LC services to share a physical
node without QoS violations. iGC tackles the interference between
GC threads and the worker threads of LC services, as well as the
interference due to HT.
SMT Optimization. Several studies [21, 33, 53] focus on optimiz-
ing application performance running on SMT servers. Deng [21]
proposes a symbiotic scheduling approach to solve the resource
contention between virtual machines on SMT processors. vsmt-
io [53] proposes to improve I/O performance and efficiency on SMT
processors in virtualized clouds. iGC proposes to place different
kinds of threads onto different CPUs while taking advantage of
memory hierarchy.
7 CONCLUSION
iGC is motivated by the performance degradation due to CPU in-
terference between concurrent GC threads and worker threads of
LC services. Scheduling GC threads and worker threads onto sep-
arate processing units opens up the opportunity for performance
improvement of LC services. Furthermore, iGC tackles HT interfer-
ence by placing worker threads and batch threads onto different
cores, but also takes advantage of HT capability by prioritizing the
placement of worker threads and GC threads in the same cores
for high cache utilization. iGC is designed and implemented as
a ready-to-use middleware for JVM-based applications in Linux
environments. Experimental results show that iGC significantly
improves the performance of two state-of-the-art concurrent GC
mechanisms (ZGC and G1 GC) for LC services and the throughput
in multi-tenant systems.
